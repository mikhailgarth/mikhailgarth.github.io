How to order beer in Prague (if you are not Czech)

The Vltava river runs through Prague and alongside the river there is a promenade and quay where boats and boat hotels moor. The wall above the promenade has bars which are set inside and open out to the promenade with huge circular doors which can swivel open on warm days.

I was sitting outside one of these bars with my son, and next to us there was a large part of young German men. What struck me when the Czech waitress came to take their orders was that they had to speak to her in English. Germany is the huge neighbor to the north of the Czech Republic and, under the Hapsburgs, Prague used to be a predominantly German-speaking city. I might expect that German would be the second language but a series of momentous historical accidents over hundreds of years dictated that it would not be.

Contrast the dominance of English with its ancient obscure origins and how it was a junior partner to Latin in the Middle Ages. Caedmon was a 7th-century illiterate herdsman at Whitby Abbey in northern England who, according to the monk Bede, received the miraculous gift of poetry through a divine dream. The only work that survives with certainty is Caedmon’s Hymn, a nine-line Old English poem praising God as Creator — the oldest known named poem in the English language.

Researchers from Trinity College Dublin very recently found a previously unknown manuscript of the hymn in Rome’s National Central Library — not the Vatican, though close. Dating to between 800 and 830 AD, it is the third oldest surviving copy of the poem. What sets it apart from the two older copies (held in Cambridge and St. Petersburg) is that those earlier manuscripts have the Old English text only as a marginal addition, whereas this newly found manuscript embeds the Old English poem directly into the main Latin text of Bede’s Ecclesiastical History. This suggests that readers of the time genuinely prized the Old English poetry for its own sake, not merely as a footnote to the Latin. The manuscript was digitized at the request of medieval historian Elisabetta Magnanti, and the findings were published just weeks ago by Cambridge University Press.

Old English survived the invasions of the Vikings but then it was subjugated by Norman French when William the Conqueror imposed a new ruling class on the Anglo-Saxon population of England.

For roughly 300 years after the Norman Conquest, England became effectively trilingual:

• Latin — the Church and formal scholarship

• Norman French — the aristocracy, law, and royal court

• Old English — the common people, peasants, and serfs

Grammar simplified so that Old English lost its declensions and conjugations which German retains, while at the same time, it acquired several layers of vocabulary that make it so rich and expressive today.

Examples of multi-faceted vocabulary are the names of animals and their meat such as cow and beef or pig and pork. The Saxon farmer tended the cow or pig and his Norman lord sat down to eat beef or pork in his manor. Then there are synonyms such as begin and commence, ask and inquire, or holy and sacred. Begin, ask and holy are humble as befits the Saxon under class, while commence, inquire and sacred are more fitting for the powerful and educated Norman upper class.

English gradually re-emerged over the centuries. Modern Standard English descends primarily from the East Midlands dialect of Middle English, specifically the variety spoken in and around London, Oxford, and Cambridge in the 14th and 15th centuries.

The East Midlands triangle — London, Oxford, Cambridge — was the economic and intellectual heart of medieval England. London was the largest city, the main port, and the center of trade. The dialect spoken there carried natural prestige simply by association with wealth and power. Merchants, lawyers, and administrators from across England gravitated to London and absorbed its speech patterns.

Chaucer wrote the Canterbury Tales in the 1380s in English using the London dialect. This helped establish English as more than a peasants’ tongue. Caxton’s printing press (1476) helped standardize spelling and grammar across England. Shakespeare’s works and the King James Bible elevated its prestige and sophistication.

This extraordinary hybrid then became a world language through the expansion of the British Empire from the 1600s to the 1900s, the rise of America, the spread of soft power through Hollywood, the BBC, rock and pop music, and television and advertising.

The final step has been the use of English in technology, science, business and aviation through the internet. When companies cooperate internationally, English is usually the medium of communication.

Take Airbus as an example. When the consortium was formed in 1970, it brought together French, German, British and Spanish engineers.

None of the four national groups shared a common language. French and German engineers couldn’t work together in either’s native tongue — it would have been politically unacceptable and practically unworkable. British involvement made English the natural neutral solution, but even in facilities where no native English speakers were present, English became the working language. French and German engineers would conduct meetings, write technical documents, and collaborate entirely in English as a neutral medium.

Today the entire global aviation industry — air traffic control, pilot communications, maintenance manuals, international certification — operates in English, regardless of where the planes are built or where they fly.

In semiconductor manufacturing, multinational engineering teams routinely include Japanese, Korean, Dutch, American, Taiwanese, and German specialists on the same project. When ASML (Dutch) supplies equipment to Samsung (Korean) and they need to jointly debug a lithography process, the engineers on both sides communicate in English — often haltingly, on both sides — because there is simply no alternative.

English today belongs to no one nation. There are more non-native English speakers than native ones (estimates suggest roughly 1.5 billion speakers, with natives being a minority). Indian English, Nigerian English, Singaporean English, and dozens of other varieties have developed their own rules, idioms, and literature.

English succeeded partly because it’s so absorbent — as we saw with the Normans, it happily swallows foreign vocabulary. It joyfully embraces new words as they arise so that, for example, we have bungalows for single-story houses in England and wear pyjamas in bed thanks to the British in India. It has no official governing body (unlike French, which has the Académie française) policing its purity, so it adapts freely to local needs.

The irony is that the language once stamped out of official use by French-speaking conquerors became the closest thing humanity has ever had to a universal tongue — not through any inherent superiority, but through an extraordinary chain of historical accidents, military conquests, economic dominance, and cultural export.

When building airliners or semiconductors or simply ordering a beer by the riverside in Prague, English is how the people of the world talk to each other. I wonder what Caedmon or Bede would have thought. They broke new ground in writing down English as a devotional language which could take its place alongside Latin.

I think they would have been astounded at its transformation into the common language that a Czech waitress, young German tourists and an elderly Englishman with his son used to enjoy beer by the side of the Vltava river.
