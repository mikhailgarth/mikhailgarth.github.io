‘Living is not for the weak’, says Anatoly Knyazev to Oliver Queen in the Arrow drama series.

Life is a struggle. It is hard so be hard. But that is what makes you strong.   Adversity is the making of the Arrow. Oliver Queen transforms from being the spoilt son of a billionaire to superhero Arrow who wreaks revenge on the wealthy criminal class who ruin his home Starling City.   This is a modern fairy-tale based on a truth known since the beginning of history.  We can review how this has been framed over the millennia by taking two men who set their thoughts on paper (or parchment) and left us their perspectives.

Mark Manson is a star blogger living in New York who has over two million readers.  His self- help book The Subtle Art of Not Giving a F*ck is deliberately written to be the antithesis of feel-good, positive thinking, self-esteem self-help texts and social media.  Subtle Art is a commercial trade book, written for mass appeal, with jokes, profanity, and a deliberately anti-self-help marketing hook. It's performing accessibility in a way Marcus Aurelius never had to.

Marcus Aurelius was a Roman Emperor from 161 to 180 AD — nearly two decades.  He had to deal with plague and constant war on the Danube frontier fighting Germanic tribes.  His Meditations are a series of exhortations to himself to be the best leader and the best man he could be. He never meant them to be seen by others or published.  Meditations is a private journal never intended for publication — unpolished, repetitive, written by a man literally trying to talk himself into being good while running an empire and dying of plague-adjacent illness in a military camp. There's no audience to entertain.

Neither author said that living is not for the weak, but they say or imply that it requires different kinds of endurance, patience, strength and courage.  For Manson living is easier if you have good values and Marcus Aurelius says that you need faith in a divine authority.
Manson writes that life is composed of failures, loss, regrets and even death.  Some find escape from psychological pain in highs: addiction, blaming other people, outrage and indignation, doom-scrolling on social media, climbing mountains, sky-diving.  But it is more fulfilling to confront problems than avoid them or dull the pain.  Happiness requires struggle.  It comes from solving problems.

He says this as an antidote to our current culture of entitlement, victimhood and narcissism; self-esteem to the point where failure is never admitted, nobody can have a different opinion to you and nobody can say no to you.

Unlike Manson, Marcus Aurelius does not need to challenge the prevailing culture of his society.  He shares in the Stoic idea that life is struggle even if he may be half-joking when he says it is a struggle to get out of bed each morning.  Struggle is the natural condition, not an aberration. When he wrote this passage, he was probably in a military camp in the Danube province of the empire, fighting Germanic tribes — the Marcomanni and Quadi.   From Meditations, Book 5, Section 20:

"Our actions may be impeded, but there can be no impeding our intentions or our dispositions. Because we can accommodate and adapt. The mind adapts and converts to its own purposes the obstacle to our acting. The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way."

What does not kill you makes you stronger.  He is making a distinction between external actions (which can be blocked) and internal intention (which cannot). The obstacle redirects the what, but not the will.

The universe is rational, and a rational universe would not produce beings capable of virtue and then give them nothing to exercise it against. Struggle, for Aurelius, is a kind of gift — the necessary condition for becoming what you are meant to be.  This reminds me of Manson’s idea that happiness comes from solving problems.

The overall posture is neither pessimism nor cheerfulness, but something more like clear-eyed acceptance with active engagement — life is hard, it is always going to be hard, and that hardness is precisely what makes a well-lived life possible.
Both authors see death as a way to put pain and struggle in perspective.

Manson writes about the death of his teenage friend who drowns in a lake by a condo where they are at a party.  He describes it as a seminal event, a turning point in his life.  As a reaction to this tragedy, he gets his own life in order.  
He ends his book with reflections on sitting on the edge of a cliff that overlooks the Cape of Good Hope in South Africa.  One slip or mistake and he would plunge down the cliff to certain death.  An onlooker thinks he is contemplating suicide.  Confronting mortality obliterates superficial values in life.  Death keeps life in perspective.  Death encourages us to adopt good values.

Marcus Aurelius speaks of the transience of life.   From Meditations, Book 4, Section 48:

Human lives are brief and trivial.  Yesterday a blob of semen, tomorrow embalming fluid, ash.

The imminent prospect of death keeps pain, struggle and suffering in perspective.  Good values help the struggle and make living less onerous.  If you live in harmony with others and treat them as you would like to be treated, you are less likely to be in conflict and therefore you will suffer less.

For Manson, good values are based in reality, socially constructive, immediate and controllable.  Bad values are socially destructive and neither immediate nor controllable.

Examples of good values are honesty, curiosity, charity, humility and creativity.  Bad values are popularity, dominance through manipulation or violence, indiscriminate pleasure.

For Marcus, virtue is the only true good. Everything else — wealth, health, status, pleasure, long life, even the survival of Rome — falls into the category the Stoics called adiaphora: indifferents. You can take them or leave them.

Manson’s values do not come from outside the individual. There is no cosmic rational order to discover. You choose your own values, and the only test is whether you are willing to suffer for them. This is a much more relativistic, modern, existentialist move.  Marcus Aurelius would likely see it as dangerously ungrounded.

Marcus Aurelius’s values come from the logos — a rational cosmic order that exists independent of him, which he is trying to align with. His values are not his invention; he's discovering them by reasoning correctly about nature.

Meditations is saturated with duty — to fellow citizens, to the empire, to humankind as a rational community ("what's not good for the hive isn't good for the bee"). Marcus Aurelius's self-discipline exists largely in service of his obligations to others.

Both use mortality as a clarifying device, but differently. Marcus Aurelius returns to death constantly as a memento mori meant to dissolve vanity and ambition — a near-daily meditation. Manson uses mortality more as a closing rhetorical device — a final chapter payoff rather than the structural backbone of the whole work.

Both share the view that ego and unchecked appetite are the primary sources of human failure.

Marcus Aurelius is almost entirely interior.  He is a man wrestling with his own mind.  Manson, despite the breezy exterior, is also fundamentally about inner values and inner choices.  Both would agree that human beings tend to ego, appetite and reaction, and that they need some kind of pressure to turn towards something larger and more stable.

The disagreement is about what that larger thing is —God, or self-constructed meaning.  Marcus Aurelius says the larger thing is given — it exists whether you acknowledge it or not. Manson says the larger thing is chosen — and that's what makes him distinctly modern, almost existentialist, in a way that ancient sources are not.  This larger thing is not discussed in the Arrow but it is implied and pervasive.

Maybe the larger thing comes from the quasi-religious experience offered by the Arrow.  We escape from the banality of our everyday existence by living with billionaires who have superhuman powers in a fantasy universe.  They are not restrained by paying the bills or the normal constraints of the human body.  They are engaged in a life and death crusade for good against evil and the Arrow is the saviour.  However, they do have to deal with injury, suffering and even torture.

Anatoly Knyazev and Oliver Queen both know pain only too well.  Oliver does acknowledge that it has played a part in his growth as a person over the 5 years he spends on the island of Lian Yu.

There is no mention of God in the Arrow series but it is an intensely moral drama.  Oliver, Felicity Smoake, Thea Queen, Diggle and Roy Harper all think about and act on fundamental values such as friendship, loyalty, courage and sacrifice.  If one of them is captured by a villain, they do not hesitate to formulate and execute a rescue plan. They are acutely aware of the moral need to help the weak. They go to extraordinary lengths to save Starling City and protect the innocent from crime gangs and earthquakes.  They are also aware of the destruction caused by secrets and lies.  The Queen family has a large inventory of secrets and lies.

Even the villains are intensely moral in that they know they are violating the natural order and make no apology for it.

The Arrow team do not seem to have read Manson’s book because they do not work out their own values each internally. They share the same values.  They all agree on their values even if they sometimes disagree on how to interpret and apply them.   They do not explicitly acknowledge the divine as the source of their morality.  They are acutely aware of pain and suffering, and danger and death, but this is not what informs their morality either.

God is implied in the Arrow by the power of the values they live by but is not openly spoken of.  The Arrow team do not reflect on morality in general in the abstract but they do reflect on their own actions and their significance for good or evil.   Manson and the Arrow team show that moral seriousness can exist without explicit divine authority.

I like to think that Marcus Aurelius would have approved.
